## 1. Evidence extraction grid

Analyze a source with this grid:

| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who is being addressed, and at what life state? |
| Public desire | What does the audience say it wants? |
| Behavior | What observable actions contradict that desire? |
| Hidden protection | What belief, fantasy, identity, or comfort does inaction preserve? |
| Enemy | What behavior or mechanism is personified? |
| Pressure source | Shame, competition, time, responsibility, regret, status, mortality, or another force? |
| Hope source | Why is change still possible? |
| Action | What is the audience asked to do now? |
| Identity | Who will the audience become by doing it? |
| Images | Which image systems recur? |
| Voice | Who is allowed to speak with this authority? |
| Unsupported claims | Which autobiographical or universal claims require removal? |

## 2. Loop mapping

Mark each passage as one or more functions:

- H: hook or re-hook
- M: mirror scene
- N: naming or interpretation
- P: pressure or future loss
- R: rebound hope
- A: action demand
- I: identity elevation
- S: story evidence
- X: repetition without added value

A strong long source often contains many H-M-P-R-A-I loops. The analytical goal is to discover the loops, not summarize topics only.

## 3. Repetition audit

Classify repeated material:

1. **Useful return**: repeats the thesis with a new motive, scene, consequence, or image.
2. **Rhythmic return**: repeats briefly to preserve oral momentum.
3. **Empty return**: changes adjectives or profanity without adding value.
4. **Identity reinforcement**: repeats a role choice so the audience rehearses it.
5. **Fatigue risk**: repeats pressure after the audience already understood and before hope appears.

When shortening, preserve useful and identity returns, compress rhythmic returns, and remove empty returns.

## 4. Atomic split method

Do not cut at arbitrary timestamps. Identify each minimum atom by its hidden conflict.

A valid atom can answer:

- What exact behavior is exposed?
- What does that behavior protect?
- What future does it create?
- What one action interrupts it?

Possible atoms from a broad discipline speech:

- waiting for motivation;
- busy work as avoidance;
- fear of exposing real ability;
- success responsibility;
- time leakage;
- pain worship;
- loss of self-trust;
- future regret;
- consumer versus creator identity;
- first evidence.

Reject atoms that differ only in wording.

## 5. Originality-safe remix

Preserve abstract mechanisms, not distinctive expression.

Safe to transfer:

- second-person confrontation;
- alternating shame and hope;
- future-self scene;
- identity choice;
- coherent war or forging imagery;
- repeated re-hooks;
- one immediate action.

Rebuild:

- sentence sequence;
- metaphors;
- examples;
- named mechanisms;
- action demand;
- ending;
- autobiographical frame.

Do not copy memorable lines, unusual phrase sequences, or unsupported personal history.

## 6. Diagnostic output format

Use this structure when the user asks for analysis:

```markdown
## Core judgment
[What the source truly does well and what it overuses]

## Attention mechanics
[Hooks, re-hooks, questions, contradiction]

## Emotional mechanics
[Pressure, shame, fear, hope, identity]

## Oral mechanics
[Sentence length, repetition, rhythm, imagery]

## Atomic content map
[Distinct short-video conflicts]

## Transferable technology
[Rules and workflow]

## What to remove or upgrade
[Empty repetition, false claims, harmful pressure]

## Test rewrite
[One original sample using the extracted mechanism]
```

When the user mainly wants finished content, keep the diagnosis brief and spend most of the response on strong atoms.
